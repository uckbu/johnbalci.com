Payload Interface Board

KiCad · STM32 · Analog instrumentation · AFRL's UNP

Overview

A NUCLEO-G431KB plugs into the top of the board and does the reading. Everything else — the pressure sensor, the header for the flow meter, the current sensor, the circuit that makes a Rogowski coil legible to an ADC — lives on the board underneath it. You bolt it to the stand, run the sensor cables in, plug the Nucleo into a laptop over USB, and fire the thruster.

It is worth being clear about what this is not: it is bench equipment, not the flight board. It never leaves the ground. That is what lets it be a cheap two-layer PCB with a removable dev module on top, and why the interesting design work here is all about measuring honestly rather than surviving launch.

Concretely: four measurement channels, a 12 A continuous-current target, and a 65 × 95 mm two-layer board in 2 oz copper, built to be characterized on the bench ahead of our Critical Design Review.

Design details

Rationale

Acquisition architecture

The two digital sensors, the pressure and flow, share an I²C bus. The two current measurements are analog, however. They get their own ADC pins after their own conditioning circuits. Splitting them that way allows for one of them to track a current that changes over seconds, and the other to reimann-sum over a pulse.

Pressure · U2MS5837 → shared I²C → PB7 / PA15
Flow · J1External digital LG16 → shared I²C → PB7 / PA15
Hall current · J2 / U3Series conductor → Hall output → 15.9 kHz RC → PA0
Rogowski · J4 / U4Floating coil → leaky integrator → 159 kHz RC → PA1
NUCLEO-G431KB · U1Acquisition firmware → timestamped records → host computer

Why keep a dev board on top?

Making my own STM32 section would have involved designing a power supply, a programming header and a crystal, and then debugging all three before I could take a single measurement... which is practically inefficient and expensive for a ground test board.

Power distribution

There is no regulator on this board; it runs off the rails the Nucleo already provides. The pressure sensor, Hall sensor and op-amp take 3.3 V, and the flow header defaults to 5 V. C8 and C9 hold up those two rails with 10 µF each, and C1, C2, C3 and C10 sit next to the individual parts with 100 nF apiece.

I could do a full worst-case power budget here, but for a board that spends its life plugged into a laptop on a bench, it would be extravagant for very little practical reason. That analysis matters for the flight hardware, which will be my up-and-coming project!

Pressure & flow interfaces

U2 · MS5837-02BA21 pressure sensor

This part does its own conversion and hands back a number over I²C, so the pressure channel needs no amplifier and no ADC pin of its own. It's an absolute sensor in the 2 bar family.

I picked it because FEMTA, a previous project in our lab, already used it. Once we move past the ground stand we'll survey transducers properly, but for getting a bench running, the familiar option wins pretty easily.

J1 / JP1 / J3 · selectable flow-sensor supply

The flow meter lives off-board, out in the feed line, so it gets a five-pin 2.54 mm header instead of a footprint. This supports the Sensirion LG16-043D, a flow meter that we managed to get working after altering our flow rate to around 20ug/s. This is an unfortunate change to me, because it means the accumulator is on the backburner, but its what our sponsors wanted :(

Because the part isn't locked down, I didn't want its supply voltage to be either. JP1 is a solder jumper: as fabricated, a copper bridge between pads 1–2 feeds the header 5 V, and cutting that bridge and joining pads 2–3 instead feeds it VEXT from the J3 terminal.

Hall current sensing

This channel answers a simple question: how much current is the payload actually drawing while it runs? But this is for the slower, steady measurement of amps over seconds, not the discharge pulse itself. It has to be taken without letting that current anywhere near the microcontroller.

U3 · ACS725KMATR-20AB-T

A Hall sensor is the clean way to do this. The current runs through a conductor inside the chip, and the chip reports the magnetic field around it. In this way, the 'measurement' and the 'measured' are never electrically connected.

At 66 mV/A the output sits at 1.65 V with no current and swings either side of that with direction. The internal conductor is about 0.85 mΩ typical, which puts roughly 0.122 W into the chip at 12 A. Small, but that number is only the chip. It ignores the board copper, the terminals, the solder joints and the fact that resistance climbs as things heat up. The unfortunately more accurate thermal answer comes from putting 12 A through the assembled board and watching it, not from this line of arithmetic.

Nominal transfer at 3.3 VVOUT = 1.65 V + (0.066 V/A) × II = (VOUT − calibrated zero) / calibrated sensitivity

Two filtering stages

The signal gets filtered twice on the way to the pin, for two different reasons. C11, a 1 nF C0G part on the sensor’s FILTER pin, works against the chip’s own 1.7 kΩ to roll the sensor off near 94 kHz. R7 = 1 kΩ and C6 = 10 nF then form a 15.9 kHz filter right at PA0, which both limits noise and, just as importantly, gives the ADC a low-impedance source to grab charge from when it samples. C2 handles local supply bypassing.

Rogowski integration & bias

The other current channel exists because a PMPD firing is a single, very fast discharge arc over the vaporized propellant in the thruster itself. The Hall sensor is far too slow to see its shape (the important part), including the peak, the ringing, and how long it lasts. A Rogowski coil is a loop of wire you put around the discharge conductor without touching it, and reading its data allows you to read the derivative of the current.

Why a second current channel?

The catch with a Rogowski coil is that it doesn’t report current. It reports how fast the current is changing, which is why it can be so fast and why it is blind to steady DC. To recover the pulse itself you have to integrate that signal, and that is what this circuit does, using half of an MCP6002 dual op-amp (but this op-amp will likely have to change, like said above, depending on how the GBWP affects our required sample rate).

Because there is only one supply rail on this board, the whole circuit works around a 1.65 V midpoint instead of ground, so the ADC can see the signal move in both directions. R3 = 100 kΩ sets the input, C4 = 100 nF does the integrating, and R4 = 1 MΩ sits across the capacitor as a deliberate leak. Without that leak, a perfect integrator would slowly accumulate its own offset voltage until it pinned itself against a rail and stopped being useful.

Ideal small-signal transfer · output relative to VREFH(s) = −(R4/R3) / (1 + sR4C4)R3C4 = 10 ms · leak pole ≈ 1.59 Hz · low-frequency gain = −10

Above the leak pole, within the useful amplifier and coil bandwidth, VOUT − VREF ≈ −M × I / (R3C4), where M is the coil’s mutual inductance. R4 also controls recovery after a pulse. The finite op-amp bandwidth, output swing, noise and offset keep this ideal model from describing every frequency or amplitude.

Why C0G, and why a dual amplifier?

C4 is a physically larger 1206 C0G part. This capacitor’s value is the scale factor on every current number this channel produces. A cheaper, high-permittivity ceramic would change value with temperature and applied voltage, and the measurement would drift with it: as you can likely imagine, no good! Even with the good part, a 1% resistor and a 5% capacitor already stack up to about ±6% worst-case error before the coil and amplifier add their own, so this channel is calibrated against a reference.

The other half of the op-amp, U4B, generates the 1.65 V midpoint reference I was just talking about. R5 and R6 split 3.3 V in half, C5 = 1 µF filters that node, and the buffer keeps the signal circuit from tugging the divider around when it swings. The divider costs about 165 µA and its filter pole lands near 31.8 Hz. Resistor tolerance alone can move the midpoint about 16.5 mV off center, which is another thing calibration absorbs rather than something to optimize with better, bigger, and more expensive parts.

Layout, mechanics & assembly

Layout decisionReason & tradeoff
Two copper layers; specified 2 oz outer copper; 1.6 mm FR4Broad copper supports the 12 A thermal target without a multilayer stack. Copper weight must be specified in fabrication; current capacity remains a thermal test result.
IP_IN / IP_OUT pours on both sidesStitching vias and all primary pads share the current path. Direct pad connections avoid narrow thermal-relief spokes, but large copper areas increase assembly heating demands.
Low-voltage ground kept outside the primary sectionProvides the sensor circuitry with a common return while preserving the intended isolation boundary. Bypass placement and analog return routing remain important to noise performance.
24 × 1.5 mm nonplated slot beneath U3Lengthens surface paths between primary and low-voltage areas. It is routed on Edge.Cuts and must be recognized as an internal cutout by the fabricator.
7 mm clearance; 8 mm creepage; 2 mm primary-to-edge spacingExplicit custom rules make the chosen geometry checkable. They are layout targets, not a standards certification or a complete high-voltage acceptance.
Four 3.2 mm M3 nonplated holesControlled mounting locations with copper keepouts. The lower pair has enlarged keepouts for the nearby primary domain; metal hardware must fit the documented 7 mm diameter envelope.
Top-side SMD populationOne-sided placement simplifies assembly and inspection. The lab separately fits the Nucleo and connectors, keeping the controller serviceable.
Five labeled schematic blocks and named netsPower, pressure/I²C, flow, Hall and Rogowski paths are easier to trace. Visible reference designators connect the layout to the component register without crowding the silkscreen with every specification.
Project-local sensor footprints and exact connector partsPin mapping, drill sizes and package geometry follow the selected devices. A similar family name is insufficient for a drop-in substitution.

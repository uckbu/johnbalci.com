PCM Map Generator

Telemetry frame layout as a constrained optimization problem.

Flight test instrumentation engineers lay out PCM telemetry frames by hand: a grid of measurement words that has to satisfy IRIG-106 Chapter 4, hit every channel’s sample rate, and keep every sample evenly spaced in time. This tool can be interpreted as an automatic solver to the previously manual process that all telemetry engineers would have to undergo.

01

Theoretical foundations

Fundamentally two issues that hide behind 'one' problem.

A PCM telemetry stream is a fixed, endlessly repeating rectangle of words. Instrumentation engineers decide the rectangle’s shape and then decide which sensor lands in which cell. Traditionally done by hand for a realistic aircraft instrumentation list, depending on the project size is generally a 20–40 hour exercise in bookkeeping. A mistake surfacing on the actual range can cost literal millions if it delays a flight test.

The task decomposes cleanly into two phases that need very different algorithms. The first is a bounded optimization search over grid shapes, and the second is a constraint satisfaction problem over the cells of the winning grid.

Given \(G\) measurement groups, each with a channel count \(c_i\) and a minimum sample rate \(s_i\),a target bit rate, and a word width \(w\), you must now find the grid \((M, W)\) that carries every channel within IRIG-106 limits and maximises a composite quality score; then assign every measurement word a periodically spaced set of cells inside it.

Grid Dimensioning

If you've read carefully, you know this is Phase 1. To begin, choose the tuple \((M, W)\): \(M\) minor frames per major frame, \(W\) words per minor frame. The search is bounded on both axes by the standard, and the objective is the implied bit rate staying under target while a weighted quality score is maximised.

Implied bit rate
\[B_{\mathrm{implied}} = f_{\mathrm{major}} \cdot w \cdot W \cdot M \;\le\; B_{\mathrm{target}}\]
\(4 \le M \le 256\)\(wW \le 8192\) bits\(W \le 1024\)\(f_{\mathrm{major}}\) major frame rate (Hz)

Grid Word Placement

With \((M, W)\) fixed, read the grid as (rows, columns) and place every individual measurement word in a cell \((r, c)\) such that the following four things hold at once:

01

Periodic spacing

Every measurement’s samples are evenly spaced in the row dimension (sub-commutation) and the column dimension (super-commutation). Remember, this 2D grid is read into a one-dimensional PCM stream of data. This means that a recovered signal without periodic spacing of data words is unreadable.

02

Contiguous extended reads

A data block wider than \(w\) bits spills into extended-read companions, which must sit in the columns immediately after their parent word. The block cannot be split.

03

Overhead is inviolable

The SFID word in column 0 and the FS1/FS2 sync pattern in columns \(W-2\) and \(W-1\) are never overwritten by data. A decoder that loses them loses frame lock.

04

No undersampling

Rounding to satisfy periodicity may only ever push a measurement’s delivered rate up. Delivering fewer samples than requested is a correctness failure, not a quality tradeoff.

02

IRIG-106 Chapter 4

IRIG Standard 106 is the telemetry transmission standard used across aerospace, flight test and defense ranges. Chapter 4 defines the structure of the PCM stream itself.

A major frame is the fundamental repeating unit of the problem. It is the entire grid. It consists of \(M\) minor frames (rows), each carrying \(W\) words of \(w\) bits (columns). Three words in every minor frame are reserved overhead, leaving \(W - 3\) for measurement data.

WordColumn positionFunction
SFID0 (first)Sub-frame ID counter. Identifies which minor frame within the major frame is currently being transmitted — it is how a decoder knows which row it is reading.
FS1\(W-2\)First half of the frame synchronization pattern.
FS2\(W-1\)Second half of the frame synchronization pattern.

The major frame rate \(f_{\mathrm{major}}\) is how many complete major frames go out per second. This is calculated with the bitrate, bits per word, words per minor frame, and minor frames per major frame.

Realisitcally, these are all buzzwords to non-instrumentation engineers. Just remember that a minor frame is a row, and every row is the same length. This means that each word per minor frame is a column.

Frame rate from bit rate
\[f_{\mathrm{major}} = \frac{B}{w \cdot W \cdot M}\]
Overhead efficiency
\[S_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{W-3}{W}\]

Hard constraints

Every candidate grid is checked against Chapter 4 before it is allowed to compete. A candidate that violates any of these is marked invalid and excluded from selection entirely. These standards are universal across ranges, sites, and projects, so all hardware and software are built to adhere to them.

03

Commutation & periodicity

Measurements enter the grid through three commutation modes, and each imposes a different periodicity constraint. Periodicity is the concept of spacing a particular data word evenly throughout any repetition in a column or row.

Sub-commutation

When a measurement needs fewer than \(M\) samples per major frame, which in practice is almost always, it appears in a subset of minor frames at even row spacing:

Row period
\[\text{row_period} = \frac{M}{\text{samples_per_major}}, \qquad M \bmod \text{samples_per_major} = 0\]

If \(M\) is not divisible by the sample count the spacing cannot be even, and the stream physically cannot emit the data in that pattern, since, again, this grid simply is a representation of a 1-dimensional pulse-code-modulation stream (refer to animation). A measurement sampled 8 times in a 24-row grid appears in every third row: 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21.

Super-commutation

When a measurement needs more than \(M\) samples per major frame it has to appear several times within a minor frame, again evenly spaced, the same word repeated across the row at a fixed column pitch:

Samples per minor frame
\[\text{spm} = \frac{\text{samples_per_major}}{M}\]
Column period
\[\text{col_period} = \frac{W}{\text{spm}}, \quad W \bmod \text{spm} = 0\]

A measurement sampled 48 times in a 24-row grid appears twice per minor frame, at columns separated by \(W/2\).

In the third mode, the measurement appears exactly once per minor frame, in every row, with no column constraint at all. Time words behave this way, and rarely you will find a case where you want another word to do so as well. Sometimes, you want bus data or voice words to do this (though voice words tend to have a higher required sample rate).

The placement invariant
\[c_{j+1} - c_j = \frac{W}{k} \quad \forall j \in [0,\,k-2], \qquad W - c_{k-1} + c_0 = \frac{W}{k}\]
\(k\) samples per minor frame\(c_0 \ldots c_{k-1}\) occupied columns in row \(r\)second identity is the wrap-around gap

The invariant is strict, and the wrap-around term is the part that is easy to miss: the gap from the last placement in a row back around to the first placement in the next row must equal the same period. Otherwise the sample interval stutters once per row.

The oversampling constraint

Analog measurements carry an anti-aliasing filter, and the filter imposes an oversampling factor on top of the rate the customer asked for. The algorithm applies it by multiplying the requested samples-per-second by an OVERSAMPLING_FACTOR the engineer supplies.

Digital sources bypass this because their stated rate is their actual rate. An analog vibration channel can end up demanding an order of magnitude more grid capacity than the requirement from the customer may suggest, while a digitised voice channel demands exactly what it says and no more.

04

Dimensioning & scoring

The optimal words_per_minor depends on how many samples each super-commutated group needs per minor frame. But that per-minor sample count depends on words_per_minor, because samples_per_minor must divide words_per_minor evenly for the column spacing to be periodic.

Well... that poses a problem, since neither can be chosen first. My resolution is a two-pass allocation.

Pass 1: Size the grid and ignore the columns

The first pass deliberately drops the column-divisibility constraint so a width can be chosen at all. _choose_actual_samples_per_major() is called without words_per_minor, so it returns the minimum valid sample count: the smallest value at or above the requirement that divides minor_frames, or is a multiple of it.

_choose_actual_samples_per_major.py
def _choose_actual_samples_per_major(needed, minor_frames, words_per_minor=None):
    if needed <= 0:
        return 1

    if needed <= minor_frames:
        # Sub-comm: find smallest count >= needed that divides minor_frames
        for candidate in range(needed, minor_frames + 1):
            if minor_frames % candidate == 0:
                return candidate
        return minor_frames  # worst case: once per minor frame

    # Super-comm: ceil(needed / minor_frames) samples per minor frame
    spm = ceil(needed / minor_frames)

    # If words_per_minor is known, round spm up to nearest divisor of W
    if words_per_minor is not None and spm > 1 and words_per_minor % spm != 0:
        for candidate_spm in range(spm + 1, words_per_minor + 1):
            if words_per_minor % candidate_spm == 0:
                spm = candidate_spm
                break
        else:
            spm = words_per_minor  # worst case: entire row

    return spm * minor_frames

Pass 2: Re-allocate with column divisibility active

With a width in hand, every group is re-allocated, this time passing words_per_minor through. Rounding samples_per_major up to the nearest divisor of \(W\) can only increase the sample count, which can only increase total data words — so Pass 2 can retroactively invalidate the width Pass 1 chose.

The scoring function

Dimensioning admits many valid solutions, so candidates are ranked by a weighted composite score. The weights encode what a good grid actually means to an instrumentation engineer, not what is mathematically tidy.

Composite quality score
\[S = 0.35\,S_{\mathrm{mult}} + 0.20\,S_{\mathrm{spare}} + 0.10\,S_{\mathrm{div}} + 0.10\,S_{\mathrm{eff}} + 0.15\,S_{\mathrm{bal}} - 0.5\,V_{\mathrm{spacing}}\]
ComponentSymbolFormulaOptimising for
Oversampling multiplier\(S_{\mathrm{mult}}\)\(1 - \frac{1}{\alpha},\ \alpha = \frac{f_{\mathrm{actual}}}{f_{\mathrm{theoretical}}}\)Signal quality headroom
Spare capacity\(S_{\mathrm{spare}}\)\(\max(0,\ 1 - \lvert r_{\mathrm{spare}} - 0.20 \rvert)\)~20% spare for future growth
Divisibility\(S_{\mathrm{div}}\)\(\min\!\left(\frac{d(M)}{16},\ 1\right)\)Flexible sub-comm placement
Overhead efficiency\(S_{\mathrm{eff}}\)\(\frac{W-3}{W}\)Bandwidth utilisation
Grid balance\(S_{\mathrm{bal}}\)\(\frac{\min(M,W)}{\max(M,W)}\)Avoiding elongated grids
Spacing violations\(V_{\mathrm{spacing}}\)Count of groups with non-periodic placementHard penalty per violation

\(d(n)\) is the divisor count — \(d(24) = 8\), since 24 is divisible by 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24. A highly divisible \(M\) gives the placement phase far more legal row periods to work with, which is why it earns its own term.

The multiplier score uses the asymptote \(1 - 1/\alpha\) deliberately. At \(\alpha = 1\) it scores 0, with corresponds to no oversampling at all. At \(\alpha = 2\) it scores 0.5. It approaches 1.0 but never reaches it, so the function rewards oversampling while giving sharply diminishing returns for extremity. That is the desired behaviour: 4× oversampling is much better than 1×, and 40× is barely better than 4× while costing ten times the bandwidth.

The weights sum to 0.90, not 1.00, and that 10% gap is load-bearing. A single spacing violation costs 0.5 — more than the entire balance and divisibility terms combined. No amount of elegance elsewhere can rescue a candidate that cannot place its groups periodically, which is exactly the ordering an engineer would want.

score_candidate.py
score = (
    0.35 * multiplier_score
    + 0.20 * spare_score
    + 0.10 * divisor_score
    + 0.10 * overhead_efficiency
    + 0.15 * balance_score
    - spacing_penalty
)
return round(score, 6)

The round(score, 6) is put in place to get rid of comparison errors due to floating-point inprecision. Candidates are later sorted by score, and two functionally identical scores that differ in the last floating-point bit can sort differently across Python versions. This tool ships to offline laptops that cannot easily be upgraded, so a version-dependent tie-break would surface as two engineers on two machines getting two different maps from identical inputs.

dang hardware constraints!

Candidate enumeration

The search is brute force with a deliberate expansion step.

enumerate_candidates.py
def _enumerate_candidates(groups, target_bit_rate, bits_per_word,
                         overhead_per_minor, time_words_per_minor):
    reqs = compute_aggregate_requirements(groups)
    candidates = []

    for minor_frames in CANDIDATE_MINOR_FRAMES:      # 20 values
        # Build minimum-width baseline
        baseline = _build_candidate(groups, reqs, minor_frames, ...)
        if baseline is None:
            continue
        baseline.score = _score_candidate(baseline, target_bit_rate)
        candidates.append(baseline)

        # Explore 3 wider configurations (for grid balance)
        wider_sizes = _choose_preferred_words_multi(
            baseline.words_per_minor + 1, max_extras=3
        )
        for wpm in wider_sizes:
            candidate = _build_candidate(..., words_per_minor_override=wpm)
            if candidate is not None:
                candidate.score = _score_candidate(candidate, target_bit_rate)
                candidates.append(candidate)

    return candidates

The space is at most 20 minor-frame counts × (1 baseline + 3 wider) = 80 candidates, each built in \(O(G)\). Ultimately, a very reasonable time complexity.

05

Architecture & data flow

One button — “Generate PCM map and export” — fans out into seven stages. Validation happens early, the expensive CSP happens last, and every stage has a single well-defined output.

Stage 3 runs the entire candidate enumeration once at the 10 Mbps ceiling purely to discover the minimum feasible bit rate, then adds the operator’s spare margin to set the real target. It is a full extra pass over the search space, which is spent to convert “pick a bit rate then hope and pray" into “here is the floor, here is your margin, here is the request you take to the range.”

06

The CSP solver

Backtracking is a depth-first search over partial assignments: build a solution one measurement at a time, and when a later choice hits a dead end, undo the most recent choice and try the next option instead.

Step 1: sample decomposition

decompose_samples() turns a total sample count into a placement shape (rows_used, samples_per_minor, row_period) satisfying three properties: never undersample, even row spacing, and even column spacing. Three fast paths handle the common cases before an exhaustive search is needed.

decompose_samples.py
def decompose_samples(actual_samples, rows, cols):
    # Fast path 1: pure sub-comm
    if actual_samples <= rows and rows % actual_samples == 0:
        return actual_samples, 1, rows // actual_samples

    # Fast path 2: pure super-comm (spm divides cols)
    if actual_samples >= rows and actual_samples % rows == 0:
        spm = actual_samples // rows
        if spm == 1 or (cols % spm == 0 and cols // spm >= 1):
            return rows, spm, 1

    # Exact decomposition search
    for rows_used in range(min(actual_samples, rows), 0, -1):
        if rows % rows_used != 0:
            continue
        if actual_samples % rows_used != 0:
            continue
        spm = actual_samples // rows_used
        if spm > 1 and cols % spm != 0:
            continue
        return rows_used, spm, rows // rows_used

    # Round-up fallback: smallest spm divisor of cols >= ceil(actual/rows)
    min_spm = (actual_samples + rows - 1) // rows
    for spm in _col_divisors(cols):
        if spm < min_spm:
            continue
        if cols // spm < 1:
            continue
        return rows, spm, 1

    raise ValueError(f"Cannot decompose {actual_samples} ...")

This solution finds the smallest samples-per-minor that both divides the column count and meets the minimum. It raises the oversampling factor above what was asked for every time, but it guarantees periodic placement. This effectively happens at least once to every measurement. Before I figured this out, I would generate nothing but broken maps.

Step 2: Grouping extended-read words

A bus word wider than \(w\) bits is emitted as a parent plus companions. They are identified by regex and fused into a single indivisible placement unit before the solver ever sees them.

A parent M1 with companions M1_ER and M1_ER2 becomes one PlacementSpec of width 3 with labels = [M1, M1_ER, M1_ER2]. During placement the block occupies three consecutive columns and cannot be split, which makes it one of the most constrained things in the grid.

Still, its a necessity, since you cannot place an extended-read on a 12bit/word system thats 36 bits after the initial read. You'd be concatenating random bits to the end of your measurement.

Step 3: backtracking with forward checking

backtrack.py
def _backtrack(specs, index, grid, placements, deadline):
    if index == len(specs):
        return True                       # All placed

    if time.time() > deadline:
        return False                      # Timeout → greedy fallback

    spec = specs[index]
    candidates = _enumerate_offsets(spec, grid)

    for row_off, col_off in candidates:
        cells = generate_cells(spec, row_off, col_off, ...)
        if cells is None:
            continue
        if not all(grid.is_free(r, c) for r, c, _ in cells):
            continue

        # Place all cells
        for r, c, label in cells:
            grid.place(r, c, label)
        placements[index] = cells

        # Forward check: verify all remaining specs have >= 1 valid offset
        if index + 1 < len(specs):
            if not _forward_check(specs[index + 1:], grid):
                for r, c, _ in cells:     # Undo and try next candidate
                    grid.unplace(r, c)
                placements[index] = []
                continue

        if _backtrack(specs, index + 1, grid, placements, deadline):
            return True

        # Undo
        for r, c, _ in cells:
            grid.unplace(r, c)
        placements[index] = []

    return False

When the solver backtracks from level \(k\) to level \(k-1\) it must restore the grid to exactly the state it was in before the level \(k-1\) choice. That is why PeriodicGrid exposes both place() and unplace(). Together they form a reversible state machine, and, naturally, you'd want an exact reversal with the sort of constraints that come with PCM telemetry.

A greedy placer takes each measurement’s first valid offset and never looks back. That fails as soon as measurements compete for the same cells: accelerometer A1 wants columns 1 and 25 in every row, A2 wants the same columns at the same rate, and once A1 has them A2 is stuck shuffling into whatever is left, which is possibly nothing. Backtracking can undo A1 and move it to column 2, freeing column 1 for A2.

So, practically, the ability to reconsider makes the search complete because it finds a valid arrangement whenever one exists.

Forward checking, measured

Three enhancements over naive backtracking

The implementation adds three things that turn a theoretically exponential algorithm into a practical one.

01

Variable ordering of the most-constrained first

compute_placement_specs() sorts specs by total_placements descending. This is the MRV heuristic: placing the most constrained variable first maximises the pruning effect for everything after it. In practice, high-rate super-commutated accelerometers filling dozens of cells go before low-rate sub-commutated pressures filling a handful. Time words go last because they have no periodicity requirement, so they can take whatever is left.

02

Forward checking leading to early dead-end detection

After each placement, _forward_check() scans every remaining unplaced spec and verifies it still has at least one valid offset. If any future spec has zero options the branch is already doomed, so the solver undoes immediately rather than exploring a subtree that cannot succeed. Without it the solver might place ten specs, discover on the eleventh that it is blocked, and unwind all ten. With it, spec eleven’s doom is caught right after the placement that caused it, which is usually spec two or three.

03

Deadline timeout... which hopefully never happens!

A ten-second wall-clock deadline is checked at the top of every recursive call. If the backtracker cannot finish in time (which realisitically happens only for literally impossibly constrained grids)it aborts, the grid resets via PeriodicGrid.create(), and returns an error.

Where performance actually matters

Exactly one place: the inner loop of the backtracker.

hot_loop.py
for row_off, col_off in candidates:
    cells = generate_cells(...)                          # ← allocation-heavy
    if cells is None:
        continue
    if not all(grid.is_free(r, c) for r, c, _ in cells):  # ← O(cells) checks
        continue

    for r, c, label in cells:
        grid.place(r, c, label)                          # ← 2 array writes

    # ... forward check ...

    for r, c, _ in cells:
        grid.unplace(r, c)                               # ← 2 array writes

generate_cells() allocates a fresh list of tuples on every call. For a super-commutated measurement on a 24×48 grid with spm=2 that is 48 (int, int, str) tuples per call, and a tight loop trying dozens of offsets generates thousands of short-lived allocations that pressure CPython’s small-object allocator.

While this fix is known, I didn't have time to implement. Since this is being run completely locally on one machine at a time, though, it doesn't really matter... and I'm really just nitpicking for specifics right now.

What I learned

While the search was difficult, the harder part was finding out what constituted a 'better' grid. I mean, I didn't even know what a grid was before I got into this! Speaking to several engineers and having to get an idea of what the scoring weights should prioritize, i.e. the refusal to ever undersample, the choice to fail loudly rather than return a wrong map, etc. all had to be made while I was still understanding the principles and technology that guide PCM telemetry. While, of course, the technical difficulty was of major importance in this project, as I had never worked on a CSP project this in-depth aside from a silly sudoku solver, I'd like to say that most of my professional development came from the setting I programmed in (a secure environment that had an innumerable amount of restrictions), as well as working alongside engineers much more senior than I am to understand the requirements for the whole thing.

I'd be very happy to do it again. So much of my professional development has come from solving actual problems in real software engineering environments, and I can't wait for the next opportunity to stretch myself unbelievably thin until I find my way through.
